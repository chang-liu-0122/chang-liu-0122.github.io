Alongside the PhD I teach as an academic tutor at the University of Sydney — where I did my own undergraduate and honours degrees — across everything from first-year foundations to master's-level computational statistics.

My field is functional data analysis. The idea is simple to state: instead of treating each observation as a number, treat it as an entire function — a whole curve measured across some continuum. A day of pedestrian counts across 24 hours, a year of mortality rates across every age, a trading day of prices across every minute. Analysing the curve as a single object keeps information that collapsing it to a summary number throws away. The plot below is a real example, if you want to see what that means in practice.

Within that field I care most about functional time series, where those curves arrive in sequence and the dependence between them becomes the thing worth modelling. I also build statistical software — I wrote and maintain PhaseGMM on CRAN — because a method nobody can run is a method nobody uses.

Away from all this: I play basketball and spend a good part of most weeks in the gym. At home I'm outnumbered by Mochi and Shadow, two cats with strong opinions about when I've been at the desk long enough.

Estimating the long-memory parameter d in fractionally integrated processes is difficult: the standard local Whittle and detrended fluctuation analysis estimators carry substantial bias, especially when strong short-range autoregressive dependence is present.

We develop a bias correction based on sieve bootstrapping. The procedure applies a sieve bootstrap to data that has first been prefiltered by a preliminary estimate of the memory parameter — we recommend the local polynomial Whittle with noise (LPWN) estimator for that initial step, since it is already designed to reduce bias under short-range dependence.

Simulation studies first establish the scale of the bias problem, then show the improvement the bootstrap enhancement delivers. As a byproduct, the method also yields confidence intervals for the memory parameter — something the point estimators alone do not provide.

Estimation and inference for linear errors-in-variables models with symmetric measurement error. Most existing methods need strong assumptions — a known error covariance, or replicated observations. PhaseGMM works when you have neither.

The approach is built on a generalised method of moments framework where the estimating equations come from two sources: minimising the distance between the empirical phase function (the normalised characteristic function) of the response and that of the linear combination of covariates, and minimising a corrected least-squares discrepancy. Where replicates are available, the method combines with classical corrections to handle heteroscedastic errors and gain efficiency.

  • Works under mild assumptions — skewed signal, symmetric error
  • No parametric distributional assumption required
  • Handles heavy-tailed errors
  • No replication needed, but uses it when present

Implements methods published in Biometrika and Statistica Sinica by Nghiem and Potgieter.

install.packages("PhaseGMM")

ρ(t) = ϕ(t) |ϕ(t)| The phase function — the characteristic function normalised to unit modulus. It discards scale but keeps asymmetry, which is what lets the method identify the model without knowing the error distribution.

Linear mixed models have hierarchical structure, so outliers can appear at more than one level: a single anomalous observation within a cluster, or an entire cluster that behaves unlike the rest. Detecting one is not the same problem as detecting the other.

This thesis proposed and evaluated methods for identifying both individual- and cluster-level outliers, assessed through simulation studies and applied to real-world data.
